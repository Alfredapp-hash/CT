A garden still growing

A garden is an honest metaphor because it refuses to flatter us. It does not let you claim that growth is a matter of effort alone, and it does not let you off the hook either. It insists on a truth most of us resist: that the important part happens where you cannot watch it, on a schedule you did not set.

Rooted in Purpose is built on that refusal. It moves through four things a garden does — seeds, storms, waiting, pruning — and each one corrects a different lie we tell ourselves about purpose.

Seeds, and the indignity of being buried

A seed is put in the dark and covered over. From the outside this looks exactly like a burial, and that is the difficulty. There is no visible difference between the beginning of something and the end of it. You cannot tell, standing over the soil, whether you are waiting or grieving.

Purpose begins quietly, long before we believe we are ready. That is the first correction, and the hardest, because we want purpose to announce itself — to arrive with a clear assignment and a start date. Instead it begins as something small, placed somewhere unlit, doing work no one can see.

Storms, and what roots are for

We ask for clear skies. Clear skies grow shallow roots. A plant that has never been pushed sideways by weather has never had a reason to reach deeper, and the first serious wind takes it.

I want to be careful here, because this idea can be misused. To say that storms strengthen roots is not to say that every hard thing was sent for your improvement, or that suffering is a curriculum. Some weather is simply weather. What is true is narrower and more useful: the reaching you do during a storm is real reaching, and it does not undo itself when the sky clears. Whatever you had to grip in order to stay upright is still there afterward, holding.

Waiting, which is not nothing

Waiting is the season we are worst at, because it looks from the outside like inactivity, and we have been trained to distrust anything that does not look productive. But a dormant season is not an empty one. Roots extend in winter. Soil recovers. The absence of visible fruit is not the absence of work.

“Your purpose is not lost. It’s growing quietly, even when you cannot see it.”

That sentence is the spine of the book. Lost and hidden are not the same condition, though they feel identical from where you are standing. One requires searching. The other requires patience, which is harder, because patience gives you nothing to do.

Pruning, the cut that is not punishment

Pruning is the movement people flinch at, and rightly. Something is removed while it is still alive — a branch that was growing, that was green, that cost you something to grow in the first place. It is not cut because it failed. It is cut because the plant cannot feed everything at once, and the gardener is choosing where the strength should go.

Most of us need permission for this. We hold onto commitments and versions of ourselves long past their season because letting go feels like admitting waste. It is not waste. It is a decision about what the next season is going to be able to support.

Each chapter of the book closes with a lesson and an affirmation, and that structure is deliberate. A metaphor that stays a metaphor does not help anyone. The point is not to admire the garden. It is to go back out to your own, in whatever season it is currently in, and keep tending ground whose harvest you cannot yet see.
