Keeping what love leaves behind

Saying goodbye is never easy, and it is hardest for young hearts, who are asked to understand something that adults have not managed to understand either. We come to a grieving child with our careful sentences and discover that the sentences are for us. The child has a simpler question, and it is the one we are least equipped to answer: where did the love go?

Finding Drake’s Feather is my attempt at an answer that a child can actually hold. Mama Hen and her ducklings are left after Papa Drake is gone, and the book does not rush them through it. They talk about the sadness. They play his games. They keep a feather.

Children grieve in the middle of playing

Adults find this confusing, and sometimes it worries them. A child will cry hard in the morning and be entirely absorbed in a game by lunch, and the adults in the room wonder whether the crying was real, or whether the playing means the child has forgotten.

Neither. Children grieve in short, intense visits, and then they go do something else, and then they come back. It is not avoidance. It is the only way a small system can carry something that large — a little at a time, with breaks. The ducklings in this book play Papa Drake’s games, and that play is not a distraction from the mourning. It is a form of it. They are visiting him in the way they know how.

The thing you can hold

A young child thinks in objects. Abstractions like memory and forever do not land, but a feather does. It can be carried in a pocket. It can be looked at when the feeling comes. It answers the unanswerable question with something concrete: the love did not disappear, and here is where we keep it.

“Papa Drake’s love will always be with us. He will always be in our hearts. And we will always remember him.”

I know how a sentence like that reads to an adult who has been through real loss. It can sound like the sort of thing said at a funeral to fill a silence. But for a child it is doing different work. It is establishing a rule about how the world operates — that a person can be gone and the love can stay, that these are not contradictory, that remembering is allowed and even expected. Children need the rule stated plainly before they can believe it.

A book for the family, not only the child

This is written for children who have experienced loss, and for the families who support them, and the second half of that is not an afterthought. Often the adult reading aloud is grieving the same person. That is a hard way to read a book, and I tried to write one that does not ambush you — no sudden turn, no moment engineered to break you open while a child is sitting in your lap waiting for the next page.

What the book offers instead is a script. It shows a family naming the sadness out loud, doing something together that the missing person loved, and deciding on purpose to keep him. If a family borrows those three moves, the book has done its job. The feather can be anything: a photograph, a phrase someone always said, a game you still play the way he played it.

Going on loving him

The aim is not to help a child finish grieving. Grief is not a task with a completion date, and promising one to a child sets them up to feel broken later when the sadness returns at seven, and at twelve, and at thirty. The aim is smaller and more durable: to show that you can go on loving someone who is gone, and that doing so is not a failure to move on. It is what love does when the person is no longer there to receive it directly. It keeps going. It just needs somewhere to live.
