Grace for ordinary days

The journey of a working mother is a tapestry woven with threads of joy, exhaustion, triumph, and doubt. I put that sentence at the front of 365 Days of Grace because all four threads have to be named at once. Take out the exhaustion and you get a greeting card. Take out the joy and you get a complaint. The whole difficulty of this life is that it is genuinely all four, often before nine in the morning.

A devotional for a working mother has to be built around a hard constraint: she does not have an hour. She may not have ten uninterrupted minutes. Any book that requires a quiet room and a lit candle is, for this reader, one more thing she is failing at. So the book is arranged for a day that is already in motion — short enough to be read before the alarm has finished being resented, portable enough to survive the commute, honest enough to be worth the minute it takes.

The early alarm

The day starts before anyone is ready, and the first decision is made in the dark: get up. Not a triumphant decision. Just the small mechanical one, repeated daily, that the rest of the day rests on. There is something worth saying here about faithfulness. We picture it as a grand thing, a stand taken at a crossroads. Most of it is this instead — getting up on time for people who will not notice that you did.

Prayer on the commute

Some of the most sincere prayers are said at a stoplight with the radio on. I mean that plainly, not as a consolation prize for people who cannot manage a proper devotional hour. The commute may be the only stretch of the day in which no one needs anything from you, and a prayer prayed there is not a lesser prayer. It is simply one prayed in the only room available.

The guilt that arrives after bedtime

This is the hour the book was really written for. The house is finally quiet, the day is finally over, and that is precisely when the accounting begins. You were short with someone at breakfast. You missed the thing at school. You were physically present at dinner and mentally still in an email. Nothing about this hour is fair, because it audits a day you no longer have any power to change, and it grades on a curve set by mothers you have only seen from the outside.

What grace offers here is not a verdict of innocence. It is a stop to the proceedings. The bedtime audit assumes that your worth is the sum of the day’s performance, and that assumption is the actual error — not the short answer at breakfast. Grace says the ledger is not the point, and it says so on the days you would lose anyway.

Trusting grace more than perfection

Perfection is an attractive standard because it feels like devotion. It is not. It is a system in which your children get a mother who is constantly measuring herself, and the measuring takes up room that the children were supposed to have. Choosing grace is not lowering the standard. It is redirecting the effort from proving toward being present.

There are three hundred sixty-five entries because that is how this is learned — not in a single decision but in a daily one, on days that feel exactly like the day before. Repetition is the form because repetition is the life.

If the overwhelm underneath all this is the part you most want named, Finding Your Self-Worth, published as Finding Yourself, takes it up directly — the woman asked to be mother, partner, employee, daughter, and friend, perfectly and all at once, and what it takes to move from I should to I want.
